In New York subway stations, you often see advertising panels covered with torn remnants of old posters. They are especially common in quieter stations, where there appears to be less demand for advertising space.

These panels always catch my eye. They have obviously been altered by people, advertisements are pasted up, torn down, painted over, and replaced. But the final composition is not intentional. Their colors, textures, and shapes accumulate through a series of actions until they begin to resemble abstract paintings.

The original boards often have a yellowish surface, many advertisements are printed on white paper, and black paint is commonly used to cover whatever remains. Together, these colors form an accidental palette. If one of these panels appeared in an artist's exhibition, you might assume the painter had a strong understanding of abstraction. The composition does not feel forced.

![A torn subway advertising panel, black paint over a gold surface](/images/subway-panel-black-gold.jpeg)

These surfaces are technically examples of décollage, a process of tearing, removing, or wearing away existing layers… the opposite of collage. You can see this in the work of Jacques Villeglé, who began collecting and exhibiting torn advertising posters taken from city streets.

Décollage is an accurate term, but it takes away some of the romance of encountering these panels. On my daily commute, it is much more enjoyable to imagine that New York is telling the story of the artists who have passed through it.

The advertising panels are not only a familiar part of New York's visual landscape, they are also connected to the city's artistic history. Artists such as Keith Haring and Richard Hambleton used subway spaces as public canvases, placing their work directly in the path of everyday commuters.

Whenever I come across a panel that has been painted completely black, I can easily imagine Keith Haring filling it with figures inspired by the people and movement of New York. But the panels appear in so many different forms that they evoke more than one artist. Depending on their colors and composition, they can call to mind different periods and figures from the city's artistic past.
![A yellow subway panel interrupted by white, black, red splatter, and a patch of light blue](/images/subway-panel-yellow-basquiat.jpeg)

Its yellow surface is interrupted by white, black, splatters of red and a patch of light blue in one corner. It is easy to imagine a crowned skull appearing over it, accompanied by words, diagrams, and symbols drawn from the surrounding neighborhood. With only a few additions, the panel could become an ode to Basquiat.

Other panels bring to mind the artists of the New York School.

![A soft-edged, earthy subway panel recalling the New York School](/images/subway-panel-earthy-stamos.jpeg)

In particular, recalls Theodoros Stamos's Documenta II. Its soft-edged forms and earthy areas of color seem to occupy a space between a constructed image and a natural formation.

> "There is a strong conscious relationship between my work and nature, which finally manifests itself in an impersonal expression."
>
> —Theodoros Stamos, discussing Documenta II, as quoted in Barbara Hess's Abstract Expressionism

The quotation feels equally appropriate for the subway panel. It has been shaped by human hands, yet its final form is impersonal, produced by the commercial nature of New York.

Even panels that are still fully occupied by advertisements can recall the work of artists such as Barnett Newman.

![A Skechers advertisement and a Friend advertisement separated by a narrow black gap](/images/subway-panel-skechers-friend-zip.jpeg)

Newman's famous "zips", vertical lines that interrupt large fields of color. Can be seen here between a Skechers advertisement and an advertisement for Friend, a wearable AI device that became the subject of public mockery. Newman's zips are often understood as elements that activate the surrounding field and command the viewer's attention. These narrow spaces between advertisements create a similar effect. Sandwiched between two commercial messages, the gap feels like the city telling you to snap out of the visual noise.

People who notice these panels will probably connect them to the art they already know. One person might see Keith Haring or Basquiat. Another might interpret a three-panel arrangement as a devotional triptych. Someone else might see only damaged advertisements.

Seeing the panels as a kind of voice for the city adds to the way New York is already personified. The city is often described as if it has its own character, memory, and rhythm. These accidental compositions become another way of imagining that personality.
